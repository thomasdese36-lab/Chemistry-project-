Step-by-Step Energy Calculation Sample

For Hydrogen Red Line ($\lambda = 656.3\text{ nm} = 6.563 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$):

1. Formula: E = (h × c) / λ

2. Constants: h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s , c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s

3. Substitution: E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / (6.563 × 10⁻⁷)

4. Calculated Energy E = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ Joules (or ~1.89 eV)

Part A: Physical DIY CD Spectroscope Guide

Step-by-step assembly instructions and optical path diagram for physical experimentation

1

Prepare the Box

Take a small cardboard box with a lid. On one short side, cut a thin vertical slit (Width: 1-2 mm, Height: 2-3 cm) where light enters.

2

Darken the Inside

Line the entire inside of the box with dark black paper or matte paint to prevent internal reflections and stray light interference.

3

Prepare CD Grating

Cut a 3 cm × 3 cm section from an old CD/DVD. Carefully peel away the reflective label layer if necessary to expose transparent diffraction tracks.

4

Install the Grating

Cut a hole (3 cm × 1 cm) on the opposite end. Tape the CD piece over the hole with its shiny side facing into the box at a ~60° angle.

5

Create Viewing Hole

Cut a viewing hole (~1 cm wide) on top/side near the CD grating where your eyes or smartphone camera will record spectra.

6

Photograph & Record

Point the slit at fluorescent lights, gas discharge tubes, or flame tests. Hold a smartphone to the viewing port to photograph line spectra.

Atomic Spectra Theory & Presentation Questions

Detailed responses to all rubric presentation questions for maximum project score

1. Why does each element produce a different spectrum?

Each element has a unique nuclear charge ($Z$) and electron configuration. This creates distinct, quantized energy levels ($n=1, 2, 3...$). Because the energy differences ($\Delta E$) between orbits are unique to each atom, emitted photons ($E = \Delta E = hc/\lambda$) have distinct wavelengths.

2. What happens to electrons when they emit light?

When an atom absorbs energy (thermal or electrical), electrons jump to higher energy levels (excited state). When returning to lower levels (ground or intermediate state), electrons release excess potential energy as single discrete photons of electromagnetic radiation.

3. Why are spectra called "atomic fingerprints"?

No two chemical elements possess identical energy level spacings. Therefore, line emission spectra are entirely unique to each element, allowing scientists to identify unknown elements in crime scenes (forensics) or distant stars (astronomy).

4. How does the Bohr model explain Hydrogen lines?

Niels Bohr postulated quantized electron orbits. Transitions ending at $n=2$ (Balmer Series) produce photons in the visible spectrum: $n=3\rightarrow2$ gives Red ($656.3\text{ nm}$), $n=4\rightarrow2$ gives Blue-Green ($486.1\text{ nm}$), and $n=5\rightarrow2$ gives Violet ($434.1\text{ nm}$).

5. How does this simulation model atomic spectra physics?

The simulation uses exact Rydberg and Planck equations ($E = hc/\lambda$) to render quantized electron orbits, photon trajectory animations, and precise spectral line wavelengths corresponding to real gas discharge experimental data.

6. Physical experiment vs. Virtual simulation learnings

Combining both shows how physical diffraction gratings (CD tracks) separate multi-wavelength emissions into visible spatial lines, while the simulation explains the subatomic quantum leaps causing those individual light frequencies.
